    Same fault, two different bills. On-demand pays once: everything stalls until a client times out, then node 2 takes over and speed returns. Per-request never stalls, but every round has to wait out the 100 ms bid timeout for as long as node 1 is gone.

    Both recordings ran on one Mac (10 cores) with no network between processes, so these numbers show the mechanism. The lab study below has the real capacity figures.

    What a node computes for each incident

    Dijkstra estimates travel under five congestion scenarios, the Hungarian algorithm matches roles to responders, and a ledger holds every asset until the incident expires. Below are 200 real plans from the project's planner on its seeded 16 × 16 campus.

    Dots are responders and squares are resource units, hollow once held. Dashed lines are the campus shortcuts. Red ticks mark plans that came up short.

    All 200 land inside one lease window, so nothing is released. Under real load the same thing happens faster: holds last 5 to 30 s, which is why plan quality is the project's largest open problem.

    What the six-PC lab study found

    Three lab PCs ran the nodes and three ran the clients: 422 runs and 58.8 million scheduled requests. 417 runs were valid, and no run double-booked a resource.

    On-demand Per-request Each dot is one client setup, the mean of 5 repetitions. Lines join the mean at each load.

    Up to about 600 requests/s both modes answered nearly everything, and on-demand had the lower median latency at almost no election cost.

    Past that they split. On-demand levels off near 520 to 590 requests/s of goodput. Per-request met the 99% target at 900 in all five repetitions, then collapsed under heavier overload, down to about 17 at 9,600 offered. Why it collapses is still open; dropped peer control messages are the leading suspect.

    Plan quality is the open problem. In every run at least 93.95% of plans carried a shortfall.
